## II. Related Work

**Clinical document routing.** Clinical documents that are delayed or sent to the wrong team can disrupt communication and patient care. Communication breakdowns between referring physicians and specialists can result in delayed or missing consultation notes [3], while one study identified 305 patients with incomplete time-sensitive specialty referrals across two primary care clinics [2]. Automated specialty classification could help direct clinical documents to the appropriate teams while maintaining human oversight.

**Clinical text classification.** Previous studies have explored NLP methods for clinical classification and referral triage. Spasic and Button [6] used topic modelling to predict treatment pathways from referral letters, outperforming a random baseline. Abdel-Hafez et al. [4] evaluated 17,378 ENT referrals and found that most supervised models achieved only 33–40% agreement with historical urgency categories. The authors also identified inconsistencies between historical triage decisions and clinical guidelines, raising concerns about label reliability. Domain-pretrained models such as ClinicalBERT improved clinical inference and concept extraction [7], although Gao et al. [8] found that BERT did not significantly outperform simpler CNN or hierarchical attention models on long clinical documents. Standard BERT models are limited to 512 tokens, while long-sequence clinical transformers outperformed shorter models across 10 NLP tasks [9]. These findings suggest that model complexity does not always translate to better performance.

**Large language models.** Zero-shot GPT-4 achieved a macro-F1 of 0.86 on breast cancer pathology classification, compared to 0.75 for the best supervised model [10]. However, fine-tuned models outperformed zero- and few-shot LLMs in social determinant extraction [11] and across several biomedical NLP benchmarks [12]. The BRIDGE benchmark reported a highest overall score of 55.5/100 across 95 LLMs evaluated on real clinical text [13]. LLM performance appears to vary across clinical tasks, supporting the need for task-specific evaluation.

**MTSamples and evaluation reliability.** Previous MTSamples studies reported up to 86.2% accuracy using Naive Bayes [14], but unclear splitting and duplicate-handling procedures make these findings difficult to assess. Barman and Barman [5] identified data leakage from applying SMOTE oversampling before train–test splitting in earlier MTSamples studies. They introduced CLiGNet, combining Bio_ClinicalBERT with a graph convolutional network to model relationships between specialties. Their leakage-free evaluation across 40 specialties achieved a best macro-F1 of 0.279. Kapoor and Narayanan [15] documented leakage across 329 papers in 17 fields, while Varoquaux and Cheplygina [16] highlighted how small test sets can produce uncertainty exceeding reported differences between models. Preventing leakage and accounting for uncertainty are therefore important when comparing clinical classification models.

**Research gap.** Although previous MTSamples studies have compared traditional machine learning and transformer models, fewer have examined label reliability and potential data leakage together. We did not identify a study combining duplicate-aware splitting, independent label validation, and label-string masking within a consistent evaluation framework. Our study investigates these issues through duplicate removal, human review of conflicting specialty labels, and comparison of rule-based, linear, and transformer models across 11 specialties with confidence intervals. We additionally examine whether explicit specialty names within clinical notes influence classification performance.
