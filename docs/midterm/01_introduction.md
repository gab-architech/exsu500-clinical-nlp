# I. Introduction

Clinical documents must be routed to the appropriate medical specialty to ensure timely review and clinical action. However, referral routing remains a potential source of inefficiency and delays in care. Physicians spend an average of 5.8 hours in electronic health records (EHRs) for every 8 hours of scheduled patient care, including 2.3 hours on documentation [1]. Inefficient referral processes can further disrupt continuity of care. In one primary care study, 305 patients experienced delays in completing time-sensitive specialty referrals, including referrals for suspicious skin lesions and chest pain [2]. Similarly, breakdowns in cross-institutional information flow can result in referring clinicians receiving specialist consultation notes late or not at all [3]. Automating aspects of referral processing could help address these challenges; however, reliable automated classification remains difficult. For example, a text-based approach evaluated for triaging ear, nose, and throat (ENT) referrals achieved only 53.8% agreement with historical referral classifications [4].

Natural language processing (NLP) offers a potential approach to classifying unstructured clinical text according to medical specialty. By extracting information from free-text clinical documents, NLP models can identify patterns associated with different specialties and support automated document routing. The Medical Transcription Samples (MTSamples) dataset, a publicly available collection of medical transcriptions, provides a resource for developing and evaluating such classification approaches. However, model performance on clinical text classification tasks may be affected by data leakage, which can lead to overly optimistic estimates of generalisability. Recent work on specialty classification using MTSamples has highlighted this concern, underscoring the importance of rigorous data preparation and leakage-controlled evaluation when comparing model performance [5].

Despite growing interest in applying NLP and large language models (LLMs) to clinical text classification, several methodological questions remain relevant. In particular, performance comparisons across different model families require consistent evaluation conditions to ensure that observed differences reflect model capabilities rather than differences in data preparation or evaluation procedures. Additionally, clinical notes may contain explicit specialty-identifying terms that provide direct clues to their assigned labels. Evaluating models with these terms masked can help determine the extent to which classification depends on explicit lexical cues rather than broader clinical context. These considerations are important for assessing whether automated specialty classification can generalise beyond superficial textual associations.

This study investigates the prediction of medical specialties from free-text clinical transcriptions under a leakage-controlled evaluation framework. Specifically, it addresses three research questions: (1) How accurately can the medical specialty associated with a clinical note be predicted from its free text? (2) How does classification performance compare across different model families? and (3) To what extent does performance change when specialty-identifying label strings within the text are masked? The study makes three contributions: (1) the development of a cleaned and documented MTSamples dataset comprising 2,230 notes across 11 medical specialties; (2) manual validation of specialty labels, demonstrating substantial inter-rater agreement (κ = 0.85); and (3) a pre-specified evaluation framework comparing five models across three model families using a frozen, stratified data split.
