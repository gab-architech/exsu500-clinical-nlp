# II. Related Work

**Why routing matters.** Clinical documents that reach the wrong team, or no team, are a recognized failure point in care. Ambulatory physicians spend 5.8 hours in the electronic health record (EHR) per 8 hours of scheduled patient time, 2.3 hours of it on documentation [1]. Across institutions, referrers receive consultant notes late or not at all because of workflow and information-flow breakdowns [3], and an EHR review at two primary care clinics found 305 patients with time-sensitive specialty referrals left open [2]. Automatically suggesting the specialty a note belongs to is a small, well-defined step towards reducing this burden, provided a human stays in the loop.

**Models for clinical text.** Text classification has supported triage with simple methods: topic models built on referral letters predicted treatment pathways better than a random baseline [6], while on 17,378 ENT referrals most supervised models agreed with historical urgency categories only 33% to 40% of the time, showing how inconsistent labels cap performance [4]. Domain-pretrained transformers such as Bio+ClinicalBERT improved clinical inference and concept extraction over general BERT, but not de-identification [7], so domain pretraining is not automatically beneficial. On full clinical documents, no BERT variant significantly outperformed simple CNN or hierarchical attention models, and BERT was about 10x slower [8]; the authors attribute this to attention being diluted away from the few keywords that decide a label. Standard BERT models also truncate input at 512 tokens, and long-sequence clinical models outperformed them on all 10 tasks tested [9]. Whether a transformer beats a well-tuned linear model on a given clinical task is therefore an empirical question, not a default.

**What large language models add, and where they fail.** Zero-shot GPT-4 reached a macro-F1 of 0.86 on breast cancer pathology reports against 0.75 for the best supervised model [10], but fine-tuned models outperformed zero- and few-shot GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 for extracting social determinants of health [11], and fine-tuned models led LLMs across 12 biomedical NLP benchmarks [12]. On real clinical text, the best of 95 LLMs scored 55.5 out of 100 [13]. LLMs are also API-dependent and hard to reproduce, which matters for a course project that must run on a free notebook and be re-run by others.

**Why earlier MTSamples results cannot be trusted.** MTSamples is the public dataset most used for specialty classification. Recent work reports up to 86.2% accuracy with Naive Bayes, ahead of every transformer tested [14], without reporting how the data were split or deduplicated. A leakage-free benchmark on the same dataset reached a best macro-F1 of only 0.279 across 40 raw labels, and traced earlier scores above 0.90 to oversampling applied before the train/test split [5]. This matches a wider pattern: leakage has been documented in 329 papers across 17 fields, including duplicates across splits and features that encode the label, and in one field every claimed advantage of complex models over logistic regression disappeared once leakage was fixed [15]. Small test sets add a further problem, since the uncertainty on reported performance is often larger than the differences between methods [16].

**Gap.** No MTSamples study we found combines (i) a split built on deduplicated texts, so that no note appears in two piles, (ii) removal of label-bearing fields such as `keywords`, (iii) class labels validated by human reviewers with measured agreement, and (iv) a comparison of rule, linear and transformer models reported with confidence intervals. Our project addresses this gap, and asks how much of the reported performance on this task comes from duplicates and label leakage.
