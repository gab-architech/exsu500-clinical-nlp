# II. Related Work

**Clinical document routing.**  Clinical documents that are delayed or sent to the wrong team can disrupt communication and patient care. Physicians spend an average of 5.8 hours in the electronic health record (EHR) per 8 hours of scheduled patient time, including 2.3 hours on documentation [1]. Communication breakdowns between referring physicians and specialists can result in delayed or missing consultation notes [3], while one study identified 305 patients with incomplete time-sensitive specialty referrals across two primary care clinics [2]. Automated specialty classification could help direct clinical documents to the appropriate teams while maintaining human oversight.

**Clinical text classification.** Previous studies have explored NLP methods for clinical classification and referral triage. Spasic and Button [6] used topic modelling to predict treatment pathways from referral letters, outperforming a random baseline. Abdel-Hafez et al. [4] evaluated 17,378 ENT referrals and found that most supervised models achieved only 33–40% agreement with historical urgency categories. The authors also identified inconsistencies between historical triage decisions and clinical guidelines, raising concerns about label reliability. Domain-pretrained models such as ClinicalBERT improved clinical inference and concept extraction, but not de-identification [7]. Gao et al. [8] found that BERT did not significantly outperform simpler CNN or hierarchical attention models on long clinical documents. Standard BERT models are limited to 512 tokens, while long-sequence clinical transformers outperformed shorter models across 10 NLP tasks [9]. These findings suggest that model complexity does not always translate to better performance.

**Large language models.** Zero-shot GPT-4 achieved a macro-F1 of 0.86 on breast cancer pathology classification, compared to 0.75 for the best supervised model [10]. However, fine-tuned models outperformed zero- and few-shot LLMs in social determinant extraction [11] and across several biomedical NLP benchmarks [12]. Gaber et al. [18] evaluated LLMs for triage, specialist referral, and diagnosis, finding inconsistencies in triage and difficulties with severe cases. Similarly, the BRIDGE benchmark reported a highest overall score of 55.5/100 across 95 LLMs evaluated on real clinical text [13]. These findings highlight the variability of LLM performance and the importance of task-specific evaluation.

**MTSamples and evaluation reliability.** Previous MTSamples studies reported up to 86.2% accuracy using Naive Bayes [14], but unclear splitting and duplicate-handling procedures make these findings difficult to assess. A leakage-free benchmark reported a best macro-F1 of 0.279 across 40 labels and identified data leakage as a potential source of inflated results in earlier studies [5]. Kapoor and Narayanan [15] documented leakage across 329 papers in 17 fields, while Varoquaux and Cheplygina [16] highlighted how small test sets can produce uncertainty exceeding reported differences between models. These findings emphasize the importance of preventing leakage and reporting performance with appropriate uncertainty.

**Research gap.** We did not identify an MTSamples study combining duplicate-aware splitting, removal of label-revealing fields such as keywords, human validation of specialty labels, and comparison of rule-based, linear, and transformer models with confidence intervals. Our project aims to address these limitations through careful data cleaning, label validation, and consistent model evaluation. We will also examine whether specialty-related information remaining in clinical notes influences performance.
