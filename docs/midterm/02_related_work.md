# II. Related Work

**Clinical document routing.** Inefficient clinical document routing can disrupt continuity of care. Physicians spend an average of 5.8 hours in electronic health records (EHRs) per 8 hours of scheduled patient care, including 2.3 hours on documentation [1]. A study across two primary care clinics identified 305 incomplete, time-sensitive specialty referrals, while cross-institutional workflow and information-flow barriers can delay or prevent referring clinicians from receiving specialist consultation notes [2], [3]. Natural language processing (NLP) has been explored as a means of supporting this process. Spasic and Button demonstrated that topic models derived from referral letters could predict subsequent treatment outcomes more accurately than a stratified random baseline [6]. Similarly, Abdel-Hafez et al. evaluated 17,378 ENT referrals and reported 53.8% agreement between predicted and historical referral categories, illustrating both the feasibility and limitations of automated clinical triage [4].

**Clinical text classification.** Traditional machine learning and domain-pretrained transformers have demonstrated varying performance across clinical NLP tasks. Clinical-domain BERT models improved performance on three of five clinical NLP tasks, although they did not outperform alternative approaches in clinical de-identification [7]. Furthermore, BERT-based models have not consistently outperformed simpler convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and hierarchical attention models on long clinical documents, while requiring approximately ten times longer for inference [8]. Conversely, long-sequence transformers, including Clinical-Longformer and Clinical-BigBird, significantly outperformed shorter-sequence transformer models across ten clinical NLP tasks, addressing the limitations of conventional 512-token input lengths [9]. These findings suggest that model performance depends on the characteristics of the classification task and clinical text, rather than model complexity alone.

**Large language models.** LLMs have demonstrated promising but task-dependent performance in biomedical NLP. Zero-shot GPT-4 achieved an average macro-F1 of 0.86 across 12 breast cancer pathology classification tasks, compared with 0.75 for the best supervised model [10]. However, fine-tuned models outperformed zero- and few-shot ChatGPT-family models in extracting social determinants of health, with limited exceptions [11]. Similarly, a benchmark of 12 biomedical NLP tasks found that conventional fine-tuning approaches outperformed zero- and few-shot LLMs in most tasks [12]. The BRIDGE benchmark further demonstrated substantial performance variation across clinical tasks, languages, and specialties when evaluating LLMs on real-world clinical text [13]. Collectively, these findings indicate that LLM performance should be evaluated empirically rather than assumed to exceed that of task-specific supervised models.

**MTSamples and evaluation reliability.** The Medical Transcription Samples (MTSamples) dataset is a publicly available collection of approximately 5,000 medical transcriptions, originally scraped from mtsamples.com and distributed through Kaggle [17]. Previous studies have explored its application to medical specialty classification. Kathirvel and Mookiah reported the highest accuracy with Naive Bayes (86.16%), outperforming the evaluated BERT-based models [14]. However, a subsequent leakage-controlled benchmark reported a substantially lower best macro-F1 of 0.279 across 40 labels, attributing previously inflated results to SMOTE oversampling before train–test splitting [5]. This concern reflects broader methodological issues in machine learning research, where data leakage has been identified across 17 scientific fields, affecting 294 papers [15]. Reliable evaluation therefore requires careful separation of training and test data, appropriate test-set sizes, and confidence intervals to quantify uncertainty in performance estimates [16].

**Research gap.** Although previous studies have explored specialty classification using MTSamples, important methodological considerations remain relevant, including duplicate-text control, removal of label-bearing fields, independent label validation, and consistent comparisons across model families. The contribution of explicit specialty-identifying terms to classification performance also warrants further investigation. This study addresses these considerations through a cleaned dataset, manually validated specialty labels, a frozen stratified evaluation split, comparisons of rule-based, linear, and transformer models, and a masking experiment to assess dependence on explicit specialty-identifying text.
